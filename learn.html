How QR codes work

The 30-second version

A QR code is a grid of black and white squares. The three big squares in the corners say "I'm a QR code, here I am." The rest of the squares hold your data (a URL, some text, Wi-Fi credentials, whatever) plus extra "spare" squares that let your phone rebuild the data even if part of the code is scratched, smudged, or covered by a sticker.

What your phone does, step by step

1️⃣ "Where's the QR?"

Your phone scans the camera image looking for those three big nested squares. They're carefully designed so that any straight line through one — horizontal, vertical, even diagonal — hits modules in this exact width ratio:

2️⃣ "Which way is up?"

Notice there are only three finders, not four. The missing fourth corner (bottom-right) tells your phone the orientation — it doesn't matter if you hold the QR upside-down or sideways.

3️⃣ "How big is the grid?"

Between the top-left and top-right finder, there's a single dotted line of alternating black and white squares. That's the timing pattern — it's basically a ruler. Counting the alternations tells your phone exactly how many squares wide the grid is.

QR codes come in 40 sizes ("versions"). V1 is 21×21 squares; V40 is 177×177. Each version up adds 4 squares per side. The more data you need to store, the higher the version.

4️⃣ "Read the rulebook"

Next, your phone reads a small 15-bit strip wrapped around the top-left finder (and a redundant copy along the top-right and bottom-left edges). That strip — the format info — tells your phone just two things:

  • How much damage protection this code has (4 levels — L, M, Q, H — see below)
  • Which "scrambling pattern" was applied to the data (1 of 8)

Both copies of the strip are checksummed so that even if one is half-destroyed, your phone can recover the right values.

5️⃣ "Unscramble"

Before the QR was printed, the data was deliberately scrambled with one of 8 fixed patterns. Why? Because without scrambling, certain content (like an all-zeros payload) would produce a QR with huge black or white blobs — or, worse, accidentally contain something that looks like a finder pattern. The scrambling spreads the dark and light squares out evenly.

6️⃣ "Read the data, byte by byte"

Now the fun part. Every square that isn't a finder, timing, format, or alignment pattern is data. Squares are read in groups of 8 — that's one byte. Reading order: start at the bottom-right corner and snake up and down in two-column-wide zig-zags:

7️⃣ "Heal any damage"

The last bytes of the QR aren't your content — they're error-correction bytes, generated by Reed-Solomon math. It's the same algorithm CDs and the Voyager probes used to recover data through scratches and cosmic noise.

If some squares are smudged, scratched, covered by a logo, or wrong, your phone uses the spares to figure out what they should have been. Up to ~30% of the code can be damaged and the data still recovers cleanly.

That's it. That's the whole trick.

The rest is detail. Big QR codes get extra alignment patterns (small 5×5 markers throughout the grid) so your phone can correct for the code being on a curved surface or photographed at an angle. Versions 7 and up also include an explicit version block. And every QR is supposed to have a 4-square-wide white margin (the quiet zone) so it doesn't get confused with surrounding artwork.

Quick reference

Error correction levels

LevelRecoverableWhen to use
L~7%Clean prints, smallest QR you can get
M~15%Default for most uses
Q~25%Outdoor, business cards
H~30%Rough conditions; lets you overlay a logo without breaking the code

Encoding modes

ModeWhat it storesEfficiency
NumericDigits 0–910 bits per 3 digits — most compact
Alphanumeric0–9, A–Z, space, $%*+-./:11 bits per 2 characters
ByteAny byte (URLs, UTF-8 text…)8 bits per byte — most flexible
KanjiJapanese (Shift-JIS)13 bits per character

A single QR can mix modes — it just switches with a fresh mode indicator and length count between sections.

QR size by version

The grid is 17 + 4 × version squares per side.

  • V1 → 21×21
  • V2 → 25×25
  • V10 → 57×57
  • V40 → 177×177 (the biggest)
